We keep coming back to this point, because it really says it all.

The Outremer 55 Light was designed to sail fast, safely and with a high degree of autonomy. She could easily spend her life cruising from marina to marina, plugged into shore power and protected by fenders. That was never our vision.

Instead, we gradually prepared, equipped and improved ALCYONE so that she could remain completely independent for months at a time. Since 2017, excluding haul-outs, we have spent an average of just two days per year in marinas.

This is why we love our catamaran — and how we actually use her.

Reliability and Intelligent Design

=> Strength Through Design, Materials & Quality of Construction

I am unfortunately unable to find the original source of the lines below, and they are not my own words. That said, whether a boat is built in sandwich construction or with solid laminate, any hull that is repeatedly driven onto coral in breaking seas will eventually suffer damage.

The real objective is not to test the limits of the structure, but to avoid putting the boat in danger in the first place: sail conservatively when conditions require it, and anchor with care.

One of the great strengths of the Outremer 55 Light lies in the way she was designed and built. The quality of the materials, the robustness of the structure and the attention given to construction details all contribute to a boat that inspires confidence when sailing far from assistance.

"The early Outremers (Danson designs) were built in solid polyester laminate and earned a well-       deserved reputation for impact resistance. Their hulls had relatively low volume in order to keep    displacement under control. Today's Outremers, by contrast, feature much greater hull volume,      higher freeboard and sandwich construction; whether this is necessarily progress is open to debate.
I recently saw photographs of an Outremer 5X (Archer) whose mooring buoy broke free in Bora.    Bora last year, allowing the boat to drift into a hotel pontoon in the lagoon. When one sees the extent of the damage caused by such a minor impact, it is rather sobering (25 knots of wind, flat water and an unmanned boat drifting slowly).                                                                                                             
For long-distance cruising, where the likelihood of impacts is far from negligible, I personally.          favour thick solid-laminate construction (at least below the waterline, or better still below the.          central moulding), watertight compartments, and avoiding saildrives whenever possible—or at the  very least having a truly watertight engine compartment.                                                                       
There are a number of examples of catamarans being lost after damage to a transom or aft section  of a hull."                                                                                                                                                       

Hull bottoms... the thickness of the solid laminate beneath them is quite rare by modern standards.

Example: The Two Mini-Keels as Sacrificial "Fuses"

Designed to protect the propellers and, above all, the rudders in the event of a collision with a unidentified floating object. The mini-keels are laminated in such a way that they can break away without damaging the hull itself.

We lost one in Havana Pass, New Caledonia, at a place and time where ALCYONE frankly had no business being (see Blog 08/2019). Remarkably, we lost the mini-keel without so much as a scratch on the hull. It sounds hard to believe, but it is true. Outremer shipped us a replacement at the end of the season in New Zealand, where it was reinstalled in the yard.

Even more surprising, a Canadian owner we met there, sailing an Outremer 50, was happily cruising without any mini-keels at all!

The primary purpose of the skegs is to protect the propellers and, above all, the rudders.  

Example: Rudders Can Be Replaced at Anchor Without Hauling the Boat

This never happened to us aboard ALCYONE, but it happened twice on PAPOOSE, our previous Outremer 55 Light.

    • The first time was at Bay of Islands, and later in a similar situation at Utila, Honduras. We had left the boat at anchor and gone ashore when a squall came through. The catamaran dragged and ended up resting gently against a coral barrier, almost as if she were lying alongside a dock. It was a horrible sight, but fortunately there was no swell, no fetch and therefore no damage to the hull. The tide, however, was slowly falling and, by the time we managed to winch the boat clear, the weight of the catamaran had crushed the lower section of the port rudder. In the middle of nowhere, we were able to drop the rudder out from below, sail with only one rudder to Los Cochinos, find a better anchorage (sand, no seagrass and a little less embarrassment...), and repair it properly. The transom became a temporary composite workshop, the rudder was re-laminated and reinstalled without any outside assistance.
    • The second time was on the Great Barrier Reef. Incredibly, we managed to lose both propellers between Bundaberg and Lady Musgrave Island. As we approached the pass, I waited until the very last moment to lower the sails, eager to impress a few guests aboard who believed that only monohulls were "real" sailing boats. Suddenly, no propulsion on starboard... and none on port either. The return to Bundaberg that evening was somewhat humiliating, pushing PAPOOSE to safety with the dinghy. The explanation was simple: after a haul-out two weeks earlier, I had not tightened the propellers correctly. To remove the shafts, the rudders had to come out first. The water was murky, crocodiles were known to be around, and the job had to be done quickly. We then loaded the shafts into the back of our friends' Land Cruiser and drove to Sydney to have two new propellers fitted.

In both cases, we avoided the considerable expense and inconvenience of hauling the boat out. More importantly, the voyage continued without depending on a shipyard. Experiences like these played a major role in our decision to buy another Outremer twelve years later.

How much money n time does hauling a catamaran really cost? It doesn't always have to come to that.

=> This 55 Light is Hull No. 26, One of the Last Five Built and the Last Completed During the Lifetime of the Founder

  • As such, she benefited from all the lessons learned from the twenty-five boats that came before her: structural reinforcements to the aft beam and daggerboard cases, an improved cockpit layout, and numerous weight-saving refinements. On ALCYONE, these improvements are particularly noticeable in details such as the locker lids, for example.
  • But she also represents the very last Outremer 55 Light built under the direct supervision of Gérard DANSON, founder and owner of the yard. She was completed before his passing and before the difficult transition period that followed, eventually leading to the company's receivership and subsequent takeover in 2007 by Xavier DESMAREST and his partner.

An Oversized, Precise and Well-Proven Anchoring System

A bow anchor provides far greater precision when anchoring, and the enormous buoyancy of the Outremer 55 Light's bows makes it perfectly practical.

=> The 35 kg ROCNA ANCHOR (2017)

The original 23 kg CQR was replaced immediately after we bought the boat. Combined with 60 metres of chain and a 20 mm bridle, this anchor has never let us down. (We have 20m chain extra).

The exceptional buoyancy of the bows—far greater than on the 45-foot model—allows us to keep the anchor permanently on the bow, which is essential when trying to anchor precisely on small patches of sand between coral heads. In clear water, accuracy matters.

The chain is replaced every two or three years. 4 Pearl-farm floats fitted with carabiners are also carried aboard and are often used to help protect the coral.

The 35 kg ROCNA looks surprisingly small beneath the massive, well-supported bows.

=> The LOFRANS 1500-WATT WINDLASS (2024)

Installed in 2024, it replaced the original 1200-watt LOFRANS windlass from 2004, whose life we had somehow managed to extend by another eight years.

The new unit is more than adequate. If the load becomes excessive, a little assistance from the engines is all that is required. Windage remains manageable up to around 30 knots of wind, especially when the helmsman can react quickly using the wonderfully smooth MORSE engine controls.

=> The WIRED HAND REMOTE CONTROL (2017)

The wired windlass remote allows you to see what is really happening at the bow. Although the helm-mounted control is sufficient in most situations, there are times when nothing replaces being right next to the anchor and chain.

You still have to haul quite a bit of chain manually during the final 25 metres !

Bertrand taught us a particularly clever solution for attaching the bridle to the chain: an 8 mm spliced Vectran strop. It is extremely strong and, unlike a shackle, can be released instantly with a single pull. A very useful feature when it is blowing hard and you want to leave an anchorage quickly.

Urban anchorage in San Diego (2019): as in Auckland Harbour, you need to be absolutely confident in your anchor.

Fast Sailing, Even with a Small Crew

=> A Recent, Strong, Practical and Deliberately Simple Sail Inventory

  • A 34 m² CALIBRE NZ self-tacking jib in Hydranet (2019)

Built by David Parr, a former America's Cup sailmaker, in his loft CALIBRE SAILS in Whangarei, this sail replaced the original DOYLE Dacron jib, which was worn out after only two years of use.

Cut from Hydranet, this solent is genuinely designed to be furled and unfurled repeatedly while retaining an excellent shape. It is one of the most heavily used sails aboard, and it has proved both durable and efficient.

The PROFURL N52 furler, although generously oversized, was replaced in 2020. A barber-hauler, permanently rigged and readily available in the cockpit, can also be useful on broader points of sail.

The CALIBRE SAILS self-tacking Hydranet solent maintains a super shape even when partially furled.
  • A 76 m² DOYLE NZ fully-battened Dacron mainsail (2017)

This six-batten mainsail was supplied by DOYLE after an agreement with the previous owner and arrived already recut. It is extremely well made and robust.

The sail was cut more like a monohull mainsail, with reef clews that rise significantly when reefed. As a result, traditional reef ties became awkward to use as the wind increased. Fortunately, in 2019, CALIBRE SAILS fitted friction rings, allowing reefs to be taken quickly, safely and without the need for sail ties.

The mainsail runs on a Frederiksen/Ronstan track. During the mast removal in Whangarei in 2018, all Torlon balls in the system were replaced. At the same time, the entire standing rigging was renewed—more than 150 kg of wire in total.

Two Dyneema running backstays, fitted in 2017, complete the rig. The one not in use is secured to the leeward shroud with a soft shackle and can also serve as an emergency preventer or slack take-up line when required.

Reefing system with friction rings. We chose to reef at the mast: less friction, simpler and more efficient.

Reefs are taken at the mast. This keeps friction to a minimum and, although the crew may get a little wet, it is perfectly safe because, whenever conditions become lively, we leave the cockpit clipped onto the jackline.

We normally take the first reef at 16 knots of apparent wind, and the second from about 21 knots onwards, depending on the sea state. The boat is still more than fast enough without being driven hard.

The topping lift simply needs to be tightened slightly (see photo), as the new reef clew fitted by DOYLE sits higher than on the original sail.

One important rule aboard ALCYONE: the mainsail must never be allowed to rub against the shrouds. To prevent this, we use a proper boom downhaul (see photo), which can be released instantly with a belaying pin if necessary—a simple but effective safety feature.

Reduced mainsail area:
Full sail: 76 m² → 1st reef: 58 m² → 2nd reef: 43 m² (our most common night configuration) → 3rd reef: 29 m² (rarely used).

The three-part boom downhaul keeps the boom securely under control. (with pilot on windvane)

The first reef is easy to take thanks to the friction ring, while the three-part boom downhaul provides secure boom restraint.

The lazy bag was custom-made for this mainsail in Whangarei in 2017. Like the sail itself, it is a hard-working piece of equipment. Built with two full-length battens, it is exceptionally robust. We even used it once as an improvised sail to avoid arriving at our destination after dark.

Thanks to the mesh panels underneath, water never collects inside, and it does exactly what it is meant to do.

It is an old-fashioned, rather rustic system, but one that is simple, reliable and highly effective. There is always plenty of time, before and after sailing, to flake and look after the mainsail properly.The zip, which has finally succumbed to years of tropical UV exposure, is currently being replaced.

A traditional, custom lazy bag: simple, reliable and an effective way to protect the mainsail. 

Jordan, my outstanding crew mate during the Auckland–Vancouver passage, enjoying the Code Zero.

● The 98 m² MARSCHALL / QUANTUM Code Zero

Our current 98 m² MARSCHALL / QUANTUM Code Zero, built in Australia, replaced the original sail in 2017 after it literally exploded during our first passage to Fiji. Poorly stored by a previous owner, it had absorbed moisture over the years and the fabric had simply rotted.

  • This sail is outstanding in very light airs. During our second Pacific crossing in 2000 aboard PAPOOSE, our previous Outremer 55 Light, from Panama to the Gambier Islands, we encountered extraordinary conditions: a mirror-flat sea and barely enough wind to ripple the surface. Almost every boat reached Rikitea with empty fuel tanks. We, on the other hand, never started the engines. Under the Code Zero alone, the catamaran slipped along quietly at between 3 and 6 knots, proving just how effective this sail can be in the lightest of breezes.
  • For a short-handed crew, the furler is an enormous advantage. At night, to avoid being caught out by a sudden squall, we often sail with the second reef already in the mainsail while leaving the Code Zero set. If the wind freshens unexpectedly, the person on watch can simply furl the sail in a matter of seconds without waking the other crew member. For us, that extra margin of safety is invaluable.
    In light airs, you can bear away with confidence. The Spectra tack line allows the Code Zero to be recovered safely and without risk.

If the wind picks up, the Code Zero can safely remain on the trampoline in its UV protective bag.

although, its proper place is below deck in the starboard sail locker, accessed through the enlarged hatch.

Spinnaker or Code Zero?

When we bought ALCYONE in 2017, she came with both a Code Zero and a spinnaker.

Before leaving New Zealand, we bought an excellent ATN spinnaker sock and redesigned the bowsprit arrangement for the Code Zero so that it could be hoisted and set safely from the trampoline, without ever having to climb out onto the bowsprit itself.

After more than three years of using both sails, the conclusion was obvious.

The simplicity and comfort of the Code Zero, thanks to its furling system and UV protection, especially during two-handed night watches, gradually condemned the spinnaker to permanent storage. 

The spinnaker was eventually given away in Canada, saving more than 30 kg and freeing up valuable storage space.


=> Easy Monitoring from the Helm or the Saloon


Good forward and upward visibility from the helm for close manoeuvres and sail trimming

Heavy weather and night watches: everything under control from inside—AIS, radar watch, wired autopilot remote and timer.

 


=> Twin YANMAR Engines and Three-Blade Folding Propellers

The 39 hp YANMAR 3JH5 engines were installed in 2011. By August 2026, they should have accumulated around 2,700 operating hours. They are coupled to KM35P gearboxes with a 2.36:1 reduction ratio and drive three-blade FLEXOFOLD folding propellers on a classic shaft of 30mm .

When we bought ALCYONE in 2017, both engines were thoroughly serviced, together with the two fuel tanks. At the same time, the electrical charging system was upgraded with two 165 A BALMAR alternators, providing a reliable source of power whenever required.

their use has become much more limited (around 100h/year) since ALCYONE has been based in the atoll. The engine mounts (silent blocks) were replaced in 2019, and oil and filter changes have been carried out.

With a clean hull and fresh antifouling, the boat will reach almost 10 knots under full power. We rarely use maximum throttle, but it is reassuring to have it available when crossing narrow passes or manoeuvring in strong winds.

During ocean passages, we usually start an engine only when the sailing speed drops below 3 knots, and then almost always one engine at a time. At a cruising speed of around 4 knots, fuel consumption is approximately 1 litre per hour, giving a practical motoring range of mre than 1,000 nautical miles when the 8x20l  jerrycans are included. This was a safety for Bertrand in Amanu to reach Marquises if cyclone.

From our experience, a conventional shaft installation is less demanding than saildrives. It does not require the boat to be hauled out every year for diaphragm replacement and routine servicing. More importantly, in the remote parts of the world where we enjoy cruising, a shaft drive is generally much easier to repair than a saildrive if something goes wrong.

Single engine: over 4 knots at only 1 L/h. Twin engines: up to 10 knots with a freshly antifouled hull.

Outremer 45 Light or Outremer 55 Light?

More than twenty years later, the debate is still alive.

These classic Gérard DANSON designs have very little in common with the magnificent new Outremers being launched today, yet they continue to sail the world's oceans. You still meet them almost everywhere.

The Outremer 45, introduced after the 50 and 55, is a wonderful catamaran that has enabled many families to sail around the world. My own brother bought one in New Caledonia, and I had the opportunity to sail with him for three weeks in Vanuatu (see Multihulls, Issue 177, June/July 2016).

Less expensive and more compact than the 55, the 45 occupies less space at anchor and is kinder to the cruising budget whenever marina stops become necessary.

But there are important differences.

In light winds and under power

The extra length of the 55, together with her enormous sugar-scoop transoms, naturally gives her a higher cruising speed. She also carries at least one additional tonne of payload without complaint, but that is only part of the story.

Once the wind exceeds 20 knots

Everything changes.

The huge volume of the bows—with their greater freeboard and the extra length ahead of the forward beam—gives the 55 a completely different motion through the sea.

I experienced this myself while sailing the 45 in Vanuatu. We had to beat to windward in fresh conditions, and the water came frighteningly close to the forward beam as the boat pitched.

That is where the 55 really stands apart.

The difference is not only comfort. It also translates directly into average daily runs, which are often well above 200 nautical miles over several consecutive days, without ever feeling that the boat is approaching its limits.

What are those limits?

I honestly don't know.

Running downwind under two reefs in around 40 knots of wind, the boat simply settles onto its huge bows. It never gives the impression that it wants to bury them.

If anything were to fail first, I suspect it would be the mast rather than the hull. And because the boat accelerates so readily, the apparent wind — and therefore the loads — are reduced accordingly.

At moments like these, I would smile and remember the criticisms I once made to Gérard DANSON about the rather crude bow openings on his 50- and 55-foot designs (see photo).

In the Pacific, passages are often long, and spacious anchorages are everywhere.

For that kind of cruising, I genuinely believe the Outremer 55 Light is in its element. Long ocean passages, huge distances between islands and plenty of room to anchor: it feels as though this boat was designed specifically for the Pacific.

Whangarei, New Zealand: the bows of the Outremer 55 and the Outremer 45 side by side. The difference is about much more than length.



A Boat Ideally Suited to the Pacific

As she is currently equipped, ALCYONE is perfectly suited to extended cruising:

  • Easy to handle single-handed, provided you know the boat well.
  • Ideal for a crew of two, with enough autonomy for up to six months. The only real limitation is cooking gas, with each bottle lasting around two months.
  • Excellent with two to four people, offering an ideal balance between comfort, space and autonomy.
  • Perfectly suitable for six people. The six-person life raft, purchased in 2017, reflects the maximum crew we consider appropriate for offshore cruising.

Beyond six people, life on board remains perfectly feasible at anchor, but the boat inevitably becomes much more dependent on frequent trips ashore for supplies, water and logistics.

 => THE COCKPIT LIFE

REMOVABLE SIDE AWNING to Complement the Bimini at Anchor

    The bimini is an important feature, especially at anchor. We sailed around the world on PAPOOSE (1998–2004), an almost identical catamaran, without any bimini at all. Under way, if the heat became uncomfortable—which was actually quite rare—we could always find shade somewhere on the boat, and in the worst case the cockpit itself provided a good refuge. At anchor, however, we simply unrolled two side awnings that were permanently stowed along each side of the boom and secured their outer edges to the lifelines. They created an enormous shaded area. While in California, we even had half of a similar awning made, and it is still carried aboard ALCYONE as a backup.

    In 2017, the bimini was refurbished. We also added two removable side extensions, attached with zippers and simply rolled away when not in use (see photo). Securing them to the lifelines takes less than one minute with two people, yet they provide remarkably effective shade.They are indispensable, especially on the port side, which faces the sun when lying head to the trade winds in the Southern Hemisphere.

    NB: Two simple hook-on stern sunshades are also carried aboard to provide quick additional shade over the aft end of the cockpit in the evening.

    ALCYONE was originally fitted with a bimini supported by a particularly robust stainless-steel frame. Its design leaves the helmsman with an unobstructed view both ahead, when approaching an anchorage or harbour, and upwards, to keep an eye on the mainsail.

    Rolled up every evening, they can be deployed in less than a minute each morning. They are stowed before sailing, while other awnings are available on board if needed.

NATURAL WOODEN SOLE N A FIXED TABLE TO STARBOARD

    The cockpit sole is covered with 5 mm of natural wood (possibly Seacork®). A few planks were replaced in 2017. Walking barefoot on it is a real pleasure: it stays cool underfoot in the tropical sun and is far less prone to showing dirt than a white GRP deck.

    Originally, the cockpit had two tables, as the boat was designed for charter use. We kept the port pedestal but mounted its tabletop on the starboard one instead, creating a much more open and practical living space.

    The chairs are simply lightweight garden chairs—just like the four light stools IKEA in the cabins. They can be moved anywhere on board, including beside the helm, and stacked away whenever more space is needed.

    On ALCYONE, as on most cruising catamarans of her generation, everyday life revolves around the bridgedeck—especially the saloon and the cockpit.

=> THE DINGHY:  HYBRID 3,60m + 15hp YAMAHA ENDURO

    Choosing the right dinghy is a never-ending debate, yet it is one of the most important decisions for long-distance cruising.

    During our circumnavigation aboard PAPOOSE, we opted for a lightweight aluminium-bottom inflatable. It had to be light enough to plane with just two people aboard, which meant a fairly long hull (3.20 m) combined with an equally lightweight outboard—a two-stroke Yamaha 6 hp, derestricted to 8 hp. It served us remarkably well throughout the voyage.

    When we bought ALCYONE in 2017, she came with a 3.60 m HIGHFIELD aluminium dinghy inherited from her previous life in New Zealand. The aluminium hull was still in excellent condition, but the tubes had reached the end of their life. We therefore decided to keep the hull and have the dinghy completely re-tubed in New Zealand, choosing PVC.

    PVC has never enjoyed the reputation of Hypalon. At the time, most boating magazines predicted a life expectancy of no more than five years in the tropics. Yet it is lighter, considerably less expensive and, above all, much easier to repair.

    The obvious challenge was protecting it from UV exposure.

    Covering it with a fabric cover was never an option, as those quickly become waterlogged and add a surprising amount of weight. Instead, whenever we were under way, the dinghy was always hoisted beneath the protection of the solar panels. At anchor, we simply chose a landing place where it could be hauled up into the shade of the trees.

    For that purpose, we fitted the famous New Zealand BEACHMASTER wheels. Permanently mounted on the transom, they are both light and compact. They can be deployed in just a few seconds, allowing a single person to pull the dinghy well clear of the water without difficulty.

    Everything changed again in 2021.

    The first revolution was the arrival of the E-WINCHER, whose electric winch handle makes it effortless to hoist the engine side of the dinghy every evening. That, in turn, allowed us to upgrade in Mexico to a 15 hp Yamaha Enduro, powerful enough for the dinghy to plane comfortably with four people aboard.

    The second revolution was Bertrand's idea of applying a UV-protective paint every year. The result has exceeded all our expectations.

    The dinghy is old and special looking but still in good condition today ans very practical. We almost never have to haul it out anymore except on a slipway, something that one person can now do alone. It can accept a lot of weight and mpve extremly fast. Out of remote islands, Its unusual  shape make it very difficult to steal . Most of the time, it simply remains at anchor, even in full tropical sun, without us having to strain our backs every evening.

Over the years, our beautiful wooden oars became badly damaged. When pushing the dinghy off a coral reef while staying aboard, it is far more practical to use an Aito branch—or any sturdy stick that can be found almost everywhere on a motu.

We therefore removed the oarlocks completely and replaced them with two small individual plastic paddles that, in reality, are almost never used. The carrying handles, which had also become unnecessary, were removed as well.

Since we adopted the local habit of anchoring the dinghy instead of hauling it up the beach, there is no longer any need to drag it ashore except on marina or urban slips.

The result is a weight saving of about 5 kg, softer and more comfortable tubes, and an annual UV-protective paint job that is much easier to apply.

Nothing has been thrown away. The handles, oarlocks and fittings are carefully stored on board and can easily be bonded back into place if ever needed.

TOHATSU 9.8 hp (26 kg) was replaced in 2021 by a YAMAHA 15 hp (38 kg). The BEACHMASTER wheels help—but we still prefer to leave the dinghy at anchor now.

=> SAFETY: THE ESSENTIALS

Active safety begins with careful weather routing. It is pointless to expose yourself to heavy weather when it can be avoided. Yet, in the vast Pacific, passages are often long, and forecasts beyond three days become increasingly uncertain. We experienced this firsthand on 22 August 2022, during the passage from Amanu to Tahiti, when the wind unexpectedly increased to 45 knots for nearly two hours without any warning in the forecasts. When the unexpected happens, there is no substitute for a robust offshore yacht that you know and trust completely.
The electronics are deliberately simple and, for many functions, duplicated, with a few reliable "vintage" systems still on board.
Two APPLE tablets with built-in GPS (iNavX, Weather4D Pro, etc.). One always stays aboard, while my personal tablet comes and goes but carries exactly the same software as a backup. They are simply placed on a small stand, clipped in front of the helm or used by hand.
A completely independent B&G radar (2018), connected to a VULCAN chartplotter. Essential, especially when sailing single-handed or as a couple, thanks to its standby mode. It can be monitored both from the chart table and from the helm.
The RAYMARINE network includes three displays at the helm: Tridata (log, speed and depth), the wind display fitted with a magnifying lens for ageing eyes, and the autopilot control head connected to the computer in the port cabin and its LS hydraulic pump installed in the port transom compartment.
An interior repeater is visible from the chart table settee, together with a wired autopilot remote. Very usefull with srong weather or night shifts.
A completely independent spare autopilot (COURSEMASTER), with its control head at the saloon entrance, computer beneath the port berth and hydraulic pump in the port transom compartment.
Fixed VHF with AIS, plus a handheld VHF.
IRIDIUM GO! Reliable but expensive, with a very limited data rate—the reason why most offshore racers still carry one, and we used it until 2025. A back up now.

QUICK ACCESS TO THE 6-PERSON LIFERAFT IN ITS CANISTER

When we purchased ALCYONE in 2017, she came equipped with a large 10-person liferaft, mounted transversely across the starboard side of the trampoline. This 2011 raft perfectly suited the previous owner's requirements, but it was unnecessarily large and heavy for our own cruising plans.

While ALCYONE was prepared in Whangārei in 2017, New Zealand, we carried out a full deployment test of the original 10-person liferaft. By pulling on the painter line, we simulated the raft being thrown overboard. The container opened exactly as designed, the CO₂ cylinder was struck, and the liferaft inflated immediately, despite having gone many months without servicing. It was a reassuring demonstration of the reliability of the system.

Before our first season in Fiji, we replaced it with a lighter 6-person WAYPOINT OCEAN liferaft, better suited to our crew size. Initially, we stored the valise behind the cockpit table, which was far from ideal in terms of confort and accessibility too.

Before departing for Canada in 2019, the liferaft was serviced and installed in a dedicated canister, itself securely housed inside the original 10-person container. This ingenious solution retains the same deployment procedure while providing much better protection and keeping the liferaft permanently ready for use. Our WAYPOINT OCEAN  liferaft was last serviced in 2019. Since then, we have deliberately chosen not to have it re-certified. This decision reflects our personal experience and our confidence in the raft as it was packed after its last service. It is a personal choice based on our experience with old raft in Whangarei n on accounts we have heard over the years regarding problems that have occasionally occurred after servicing.

Fortunately, deploying the liferaft is the very last option, (may be in case of fire); we ever hope to consider aboard a catamaran. Our priority has always been to avoid reaching that point through careful preparation, conservative weather routing, and a boat maintained to a high standard.


Last serviced in 2019. No further servicing has been carried out since then, by deliberate choice.

THE DINGHY AGAIN AS A MAJOR SAFETY KEY

Easy to launch, the dinghy is much more than just a tender. It is invaluable for scouting a pass or checking an anchorage before bringing the catamaran in. In difficult crosswind manoeuvres, it can even be used to push the bow into position. One person can easily do this using the PVC tube against the hull. ALCYONE presents a huge amount of windage when broadside to the wind.

For major offshore passages, however, we always remove the 15 hp Yamaha.The davits are rated for 150 kg, but in heavy seas you never really know what loads they may have to withstand. Removing the 15 hp Yamaha and stowing it safely in the engine locker takes only a few minutes before a long ocean passage; not for our biennial 4 days passages between Amanu and Tahiti/Raiatea, we simply empty the dinghy completely (reducing its weight to about 75 kg) and secure it to the davits with two Spectra safety strops, each permanently fitted with a snap shackle.

In the tiny marina of Musket Cove (FIJI), we met an English skipper who had learnt this lesson the hard way. His dinghy broke loose at full speed, tearing away the davits and part of the aft beam. Ever since, he had carried his dinghy on the foredeck like a monohull—one of the reasons he had to rely on expensive marina berths. A harsh lifetime penalty for one mistake.In an emergency at sea, it is surprisingly easy to release the wrong clutch... and accidentally drop the dinghy. Another clever—and virtually free—safety idea came from my younger brother. Simply tie a figure-eight stopper knot behind each LEWMAR rope clutch and secure the lifting lines beneath the bimini.



Offshore configuration on central picture : the 15 hp is stowed in the engine locker in place of the 2 hp, while the red dinghy hoisting lines are safety-secured beneath the bimini.

SAFETY THROUGH A WELL-ORGANISED BOAT

A major part of safety comes from knowing your boat perfectly. Since 2021, ALCYONE has been sailed by two different skippers. For that reason, the organisation of the boat has remained strictly unchanged. Every tool, spare part and piece of safety equipment has a permanent, designated place, allowing either skipper to find it instantly, even under pressure.

Almost all tools and spare parts are stored within two metres of the cockpit. Only the items used almost daily are kept in open storage on the workshop cabinet. Everything else is clean, well maintained and carefully organised in labelled boxes or zip bags.

Essential emergency equipment—including wooden plugs, spare Spectra anchor rode, headlamps and basic tools—is immediately accessible. A fast-curing underwater epoxy repair kit is always ready for use.

Immediate access to essential emergency gear: quick-release pins for loaded snap shackles (running backstays, boom preventer, etc.), spare Spectra anchor rode, headlamps and basic hand tools—all within easy reach of the cockpit.


SAFETY WITH THE E-WINCHER ELECTRIC WINCH HANDLE

Since 2019, the E-Wincher has become one of the pieces of equipment we appreciate most. It has completely changed our daily routine. We no longer hesitate to hoist the dinghy onto the davits every time we return on board. Keeping the dinghy out of the water protects it from UV exposure, marine growth and wear, while ensuring the boat is always ready to leave at a moment's notice.

The E-Wincher also allows Helena to hoist me to the masthead quickly, safely and without physical effort. A fully charged spare battery is always kept ready for immediate use. 

Expensive gear, perhaps—but nicely designed ...who would willingly go back to a cordless drill ?

SECURITY AGAINST THEFT

The Pacific is vast, and every island is different. Fortunately, it truly deserves its name, and the sense of safety is one of the great pleasures of cruising here. 

We have never experienced any theft in the Tuamotus. In Tahiti and Raiatea, however, we always lock the catamaran when leaving her unattended. Ashore, the dinghy and its 15 hp Yamaha are secured together with a long, 8 mm coated cable lock. The small forward locker can also be padlocked to avoid tempting opportunists.

Long-shackle special padlocks are available to secure all four transom lockers if required, although we have never needed to use them since 2020 , as ALCYONE has always been lived aboard year-round. 

8 mm coated security cable for the dinghy and 15 hp Yamaha, used as a precaution only in urban area. The transom locker padlocks have never been used so far.

CREW SAFETY 

Keeping this section almost feels unfair to French Polynesia. One of the greatest pleasures of cruising here is the warm welcome and kindness of the local people. We have never experienced any security issues, either in French Polynesia or even in more remote parts of Melanesia such as Ouvéa and the Bélep Islands in NEW CALEDONIA  IN 2017 n 2018. Respecting local customs has always been met with the same respect in return.

Our approach has never been to carry firearms. Before leaving on our circumnavigation in 1998, I returned the shotgun my father had offered me, and I have never regretted that decision. Firearms create complications at international borders, are rarely accessible when urgently needed, and may ultimately be turned against their owner.

Instead, we keep a 12-gauge flare pistol ready for immediate use in the upper port locker beside the saloon entrance. Since 2019, it has been complemented by a bear spray purchased in Vancouver.

That said, a firearm can still have practical uses in some of the higher islands of French Polynesia—for hunting wild pigs or goats with local residents, or even for dispatching a large fish caught while trolling—but that has never been part of our own cruising philosophy.

Two flare pistols,  a bear spray readily accessible. The machetes are used mostly ashore—for opening coconuts!

VERSATILE HAUL-OUTS OPTIONS AND DRY DOCKING

ALCYONE must always be supported beneath the reinforced structural areas—never under the bridgedeck nacelle.

When using a travel lift, the strops must be positioned at the same reinforced bulkhead locations. Wooden spacers should be placed between the straps and the aluminium toerails to prevent compression damage.

Danson-designed Outremer catamarans are well known by most professional boatyards, but it is always worth checking that the operators position the supports and travel-lift slings exactly where they should be.

Support points: under the two reinforced structural beams beneath each hull, or at the same bulkhead locations when using a travel lift.
